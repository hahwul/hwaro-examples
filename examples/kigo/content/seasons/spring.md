## Ice that is already leaving

*Usurai* is not the ice of winter. It forms on a puddle or a water jar after the calendar has turned to spring, on a night that has forgotten what month it is, and it is gone by the time the washing is hung out. The almanac files it under early spring for that reason: the word is about the ice's weakness, not its cold. Winter ice is a fact. Thin ice is a piece of news, and the news is that the cold can no longer finish what it starts.

The older reading is *usurai*. *Usugōri*, which says the same thing in plainer syllables, is also allowed, and I have used the first because it sounds like something that would break. All three poems stand beside the ice instead of on it. Water goes on underneath; children break it on the way to school without slowing down, as they should; a scrap of cloud lies in it for an hour. Nothing in a spring poem needs to be in bloom. It is enough that something hard has become temporary.
