I am not a haiku poet by training. I am a bookbinder, and I came to the saijiki the way a binder comes to anything, by repairing one. It was a pocket edition from the 1950s, the spring volume, with a cracked spine and a previous owner's pencil ticks beside the words she had already used. By the time the new thread was through I had read it twice and wanted ticks of my own.

So for one year I kept a rule. One season word per season, chosen on the first day and not exchanged. I wrote in the mornings at the bench in Shiomizaka, before the glue was warm, and I kept only the poems I could still stand a month later. Fifteen stayed. A real saijiki would be ashamed of the number.

## On the translations

They are plain on purpose. I have not tried to make seventeen English syllables, because an English syllable is not a mora and the arithmetic proves nothing. I have tried to keep the order in which things appear, since in a poem this short the order is most of the meaning. Where the Japanese has a cutting word I have used a colon or a dash, and where it has none I have left the line alone.

## Colophon

The Japanese is set in Shippori Mincho, a revival of an old Tokyo foundry face, and the English in EB Garamond. The columns are ruled like manuscript paper because that is what the drafts were written on. The paper is the colour of unbleached cloth, the ink is ink, and the seal is the only red in the book.

Nao Kirishima, Shiomizaka
