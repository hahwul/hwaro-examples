Architectural acoustics in northern urban spaces undergo subtle yet measurable transformations as winter frost sets in. Fired clay brick, a porous building material widely used in northern industrial and residential construction, exhibits temperature-dependent changes in sound absorption and surface scattering coefficients.

This paper documents acoustic field measurements conducted in narrow brick alleyways across industrial port districts during late autumn freeze-thaw cycles, evaluating how sub-zero temperatures alter street-level soundscapes.

### Porosity and Moisture Freezing Mechanics

Fired clay brick contains a complex network of interconnected pores resulting from moisture evaporation during kiln firing. In ambient conditions above freezing, these porous surfaces absorb airborne acoustic energy, particularly in mid-range sound frequencies between five hundred and two thousand hertz.

When atmospheric humidity saturates the outer three millimeters of brickwork and subsequently freezes, ice expands within the capillary pores. This pore-freezing process transforms the normally porous ceramic surface into a rigid, non-absorptive boundary layer. Acoustic absorption coefficients drop by up to forty percent across key speech frequencies.

### Reverberation Shifts in Urban Canyons

In narrow urban alleyways bounded by four-story brick facades, the suppression of surface sound absorption causes a noticeable increase in reverberation time. Field impulse testing demonstrates that early morning reverberation times in frozen alleyways increase from 0.85 seconds to 1.15 seconds under sub-zero conditions.

Footsteps, distant traffic noise, and voices echo with heightened clarity off the hardened brick boundaries. This acoustic sharpening coincides with early morning visual conditions, where low-angled sunlight illuminates frost-covered brick edges, creating a unified sensory environment characteristic of northern urban winter mornings.
