Maritime port cities in northern Europe experience prolonged periods of cloud cover and drizzle throughout November. Under these meteorological conditions, building roofs become the dominant visual plane when viewed from surrounding hills or elevated urban walkways. Slate roofing shingles, harvested from metamorphic rock formations, exhibit dramatic changes in optical reflectivity when wetted by autumn rain.

This paper presents photometric field measurements of natural slate roofs in Atlantic port settlements, quantifying the impact of water film thickness on urban albedo and streetscape illumination.

### Metamorphic Cleavage and Specular Angles

Natural roofing slate is formed through high-pressure metamorphosis of clay and volcanic ash, producing a dense rock structure characterized by parallel foliation planes. When split into thin roofing tiles, these cleavage planes reveal a fine crystalline surface composed of quartz, muscovite mica, and chlorite minerals.

When dry, dark blue-grey slate absorbs up to eighty-five percent of incoming solar radiation. However, when a continuous film of rainwater coats the shingles, the surface transitions from a diffuse absorber to a highly directional specular mirror. At low angles of reflection, wet slate roofs reflect up to sixty percent of diffuse sky vault radiation toward the urban street below.

### Urban Reflectance in Maritime Rain

In dense historic cities where building height-to-street-width ratios exceed two-to-one, ground-level daylight is severely constrained during overcast winter days. Wet slate roofs act as secondary light sources, reflecting the brighter zenith sky down into narrow street lanes and interior courtyards.

Photometric mapping indicates that steep roof pitches between forty-five and sixty degrees are particularly effective at directing wet specular reflections toward pedestrian zones. The resulting visual effect transforms dark urban rooftops into gleaming metallic facets, brightening the grey ambient atmosphere of late November afternoons.
