Late autumn alters the optical density of northern cities. As the solar arc drops below twenty degrees, building envelopes no longer function merely as weatherproof enclosures; they become vast reflection planes and shadow projectors. The low altitude of November light strikes vertical stone facades at acute incidence angles, highlighting micro-textures in granite, limestone, and hand-molded brick that remain invisible under high summer sun.

This journal documents the spatial, material, and sensory shifts that occur during the transition into winter. Through precise field surveys, photometric section analyses, and material monographs, we examine how architectural form mediates the reduced daylight spectrum of late autumn. Our investigations focus on the interplay between heavy thermal mass and fleeting solar rays, measuring the thermodynamic and visual performance of historic colonnades, courtyard voids, and glazed fenestration in high-latitude environments.

Each issue assembles original monographs, analytical drawings, and field notes contributed by urbanists, architectural historians, and stone conservators. By centering the relationship between solar geometry and building masonry, November provides a rigorous framework for understanding cold-climate urbanism.
