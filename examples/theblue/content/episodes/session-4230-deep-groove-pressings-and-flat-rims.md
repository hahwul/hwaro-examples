The final link in the mid-century audio production chain took place at the Plastylite Corporation pressing plant in North Brunswick, New Jersey. Here, nickel stampers grown electrochemically from Rudy Van Gelder's master lacquers were clamped into hydraulic record presses to stamp heated vinyl biscuits into durable phonograph records.

This technical documentary explores the metallurgical, chemical, and physical factors that gave original fifties and sixties pressings their quiet backgrounds and heavy structural rigidity.

<!-- more -->

Plastylite utilized a proprietary virgin vinyl compound free of recycled regrind fillers, resulting in superior groove wall polish and reduced surface noise during stylus tracing. The pressing machines employed a heavy stamper retaining ring that formed a deep circular indentation in the record label area, known to collectors as the deep groove. Early pressings also featured a flat outer rim without the raised protective bead introduced in later industrial standards.

```text
VINYL MANUFACTURING SPECIFICATIONS:
- Compound: Pure copolymer virgin polyvinyl chloride (PVC)
- Disc Weight: 160 to 180 grams nominal per disc
- Pressing Stamp: Cursive 'P' ear mark etched into lead-out dead wax
- Center Groove Indentation: 2.75-inch diameter deep groove ring
```

In the run-out dead wax of every authentic pressing, inspectors and collectors find two unmistakable hand-carved markings: the interlocking RVG initials and the small cursive letter P stamper mark. We conduct microscope inspections and high-resolution optical stylus tracking tests comparing original Plastylite pressings against modern vinyl reissues, demonstrating how vintage vinyl chemistry preserved Van Gelder's original cutting velocity and acoustic dynamics.
