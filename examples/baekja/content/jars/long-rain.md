The monsoon arrived on the twenty-fifth of June and did not leave for nineteen days. In that weather a bowl thrown on Monday is still wet on Thursday. The cloths go grey. The shelves sweat. I threw both halves of this jar in the first week of the rain, and when the time came to join them the lower bowl was softer than I have ever dared to join.

<!-- more -->

I joined it anyway. There was a firing booked and a stack of pine going damp under its tarpaulin, and I told myself the kiln would forgive what the weather had done. Kilns do not forgive. They are the most literal readers of a pot that there are.

On the left side, where the lower wall was thinnest, the jar sagged nearly two centimetres under the weight of its own upper half. The belly line, which on the right sits where I put it, runs downhill to the left like a horizon seen from a boat. The shoulder above it stayed where it was, so that side of the jar is long in the body and short in the leg.

For a month I meant to break it up. Then one evening the low sun came through the studio door and lay along that slope, and I saw that the tilt gave the jar a direction. Every other jar on the shelf was standing still. This one was leaning into something. I have not been able to make that on purpose since.
