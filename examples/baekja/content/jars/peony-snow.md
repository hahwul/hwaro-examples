We have a word for snow that falls in big, slow flakes: *hambangnun*, peony snow, because each flake comes down like a petal. It began on the evening I sealed the kiln and went on through the night without a sound.

<!-- more -->

A wood kiln should cool for a day and a half and then be opened while it is still warm to the hand. By morning the door of this one was under forty centimetres of snow and the path to it had gone. I left it. I told myself the snow was a blanket and the jars would cool more slowly and more kindly than usual, and for once I was right. The glaze on everything in that firing is softer and deeper than I normally get, like the surface of milk just before it skins.

When I finally dug the door out and lifted this jar into the light, I found the fault I had made three weeks earlier and forgotten. The upper bowl had been thrown a few millimetres wider than the lower. At the join, on the right, the top half overhangs the bottom by the width of a thumbnail, and the glaze has pooled along the step in a thin line that looks blue in some lights.

I kept it because that line is the one piece of drawing on any jar I have made. I did not put it there. The two bowls did, by failing to agree.
