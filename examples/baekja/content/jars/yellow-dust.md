On the morning of the firing the hill across the valley had gone. The dust comes most Aprils, blown in from a desert I will never see, and on the bad days the light turns the colour of old straw and everything outdoors is filmed with a fine yellow grit. I should have waited. The wood was stacked and the jars were dry and I am not good at waiting.

<!-- more -->

The wind that carries the dust also pulls on a chimney. I ran the kiln with the damper half shut and still the fire climbed faster than I wanted through the last two hundred degrees, which is exactly where a porcelain jar is weakest. Through the spy hole, near the peak, I watched this one settle. It did not lean. It simply sat, the way a tired person sits, and the belly went out as the height came down.

Cold, it measured 34.5 centimetres tall and 35.6 across. A moon jar is supposed to be about as wide as it is high, and this one has gone past the mark by a finger's width. There are four small iron specks on the shoulder, each the size of a sesame seed, where grit found its way into the glaze bucket.

I kept it because it is the only jar I have made that looks comfortable. The others stand. This one rests.
