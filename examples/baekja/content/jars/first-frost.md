The kiln floor will take a jar forty-seven centimetres tall and not one finger more. I had tried twice before to make one that size. The first folded on the wheel, the wall going over like a wave while I stood there with my hands still wet. The second cracked through the foot in the first firing and I heard it go from the house.

<!-- more -->

For the third I changed nothing except my patience. I let the clay age a third winter. I threw the bowls a full day apart and left them under cloth for two nights, not one. When I lowered the upper half onto the lower, it was so heavy that I had to rest my forearms on the rim and let it down by breathing out.

I lit the kiln on the fourteenth of November. There was frost on the tarpaulin that morning, the first of the year, and the grass on the hill had gone white and stiff. Twenty-nine hours later I sealed the firebox and went to bed without looking through the spy hole, which I have never managed before or since.

It survived. The left shoulder is fuller than the right, by about the thickness of a hand laid flat. The mouth dips a few millimetres on the same side. The belly line climbs very slightly from left to right, and under a raking light you can follow it all the way round.

I kept it because it is as large as I can make a thing, and it is still not round. I find that a comfort.
