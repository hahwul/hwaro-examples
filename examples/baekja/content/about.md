My name is Seo Hae-rim. I work alone in Sodam-ri, a village of eleven houses at the closed end of a valley, where the road stops at my gate and the hill behind the studio is mostly pine. The kiln is a single chamber built into that slope, three metres long, with room on its floor for four large jars and never more. I fire it five or six times a year. That is the whole studio: one wheel, one kiln, one form.

The form is the *dalhangari*, the moon jar, which potters in this country made for about a century and then stopped making. It is white porcelain with nothing painted on it. It is as wide as it is tall. And it is too large to throw in one piece, because a porcelain wall that high folds under its own wet weight before the wheel has finished turning.

## Two halves

So the jar is made as two bowls. I throw the lower one first, with a foot, and the upper one the next morning, with a mouth, both a little thicker at the rim than seems sensible. They sit overnight under cloth until the clay is stiff enough to carry itself but soft enough to take a join. Then the upper bowl is turned over and lowered onto the lower, rim to rim, with slip between them, and the seam is worked from the inside with one arm down the mouth.

The two bowls are never the same. One was thrown on a damp day and one on a dry day; one sat nearer the stove. When they meet, the line where they meet is not level, and no amount of trimming makes it so.

## What the fire does

Porcelain softens at the top of the firing. For an hour or so, at around 1,280 degrees, the jar is closer to a liquid than I like to think about, and the upper half rests its whole weight on the join. Every jar settles. Some lean, some sit down, some open along the seam and are broken up for grog. I lose about two in three.

The ones that survive carry the firing in their outline. A shoulder is fuller on the side that faced the firebox. A belly line runs downhill. I used to count these as faults and grind my teeth over them. Now I measure them, write them down, and name the jar for the weather on the day it was fired, since the weather had as much to do with the result as I did.

## Why only this

People ask whether I get bored. I have thrown this one form several hundred times and I have not yet made two alike, and I have not yet made one that is round. A perfect sphere would be a different object, finished and closed. This one keeps a little of the two bowls, the night under cloth, and the hour when it nearly fell. I do not know a better reason to keep making it.
