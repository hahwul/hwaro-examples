Nobang is plain-woven raw silk with the gum left in. The gum, sericin, is what makes the cloth stand away from the hand like paper and catch light across its surface. It is also the first thing a careless dye bath removes.

<!-- more -->

## The cloth

We keep two weights: a 22 g nobang for single-layer cloths and a 35 g one for pieces that will be handled. Beside them is a little *hangra* (항라), a gauze with a ladder-like stripe every third pick, saved for borders.

Cut on a pulled thread. Tease one weft out across the width and cut along the gap it leaves. Scissors alone wander two or three threads over the length of a patch, and then the seam will not lie flat.

## Dyeing without losing the hand

Sericin softens in hot water and dissolves in alkali. So:

- Keep every bath under 60 °C.
- Wet the silk out in cool water for thirty minutes first. Dry gauze takes dye in the first three seconds and shows exactly where it touched.
- In the indigo vat, which sits near pH 11, dip for two minutes instead of five. Air it, then rinse between dips in water with a spoon of vinegar. Build colour with more dips, not longer ones.
- Never wring. Lift, drain, and roll in a towel.

Honghwa and chija suit it best. Both work cool and close to neutral.

## What goes wrong

- **Limp cloth.** Degummed, by heat or by lye. It can still be pieced, but not beside crisp patches: the seam puckers.
- **Water marks.** One drop on dry dyed nobang leaves a ring. Dampen the whole piece evenly and dry it flat.
- **Threads slipping at the seam.** The allowance was too narrow. Leave 7 mm on gauze.
